A food guide built from what adults actually eat

Sucrose turns a blood sugar reading into food ideas based on the meals of adults with similar readings, kept within sugar and carb limits that tighten as the level rises.

Why we built it

A number on a glucose meter says little about what to eat next. Sucrose gives a starting point: food groups, pairings and a day’s meal plan that are common at your level and fit its limits.

It is a student project, built to show how frequent-pattern mining can turn survey data into something a person can use.

The data

Source
NHANES 2017–2018 food records
People
1,885 adults without diagnosed diabetes
Groups
Ages 18–39, 40–59 and 60+, by gender
Glucose levels
8, from under 80 to 200+ mg/dL
Model
ECLAT, version 2.1

How it works

ECLAT is a frequent-pattern mining algorithm. For each glucose level, age group and gender, Sucrose finds the food groups and pairs that appear most often and records their support, their lift and a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted q-value from Fisher’s exact test.

A nutrition filter then checks each food against the level’s limits for sugar, net carbs and saturated fat per 100 g. Readings of 120 mg/dL and above share the same mined patterns, so what changes from one level to the next is how strict the filter is.

What Sucrose is not

  • It is not a diagnosis. The clinical band shown for a reading is context, and a single reading should be confirmed with a doctor.
  • It was built from adults without diagnosed diabetes, so it does not reflect how people with diabetes eat or should eat.
  • It is for adults 18 and older, with readings from 20 to 600 mg/dL.
Educational prototype, not medical advice. Talk to a doctor or dietitian before changing what you eat, especially if you take medication for blood sugar.
